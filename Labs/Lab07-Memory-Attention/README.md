# Lab07-Memory-Attention-in-AI-Systems

This lab explored how artificial intelligence systems handle memory, attention, learning, and information processing through experiments based on neuroscience concepts from Modules 06 and 07.

## Objectives

- Test AI working memory and context limitations
- Analyze forgetting and interference in AI systems
- Explore in-context learning and few-shot prompting
- Investigate selective attention and distraction resistance
- Visualize transformer self-attention mechanisms
- Compare biological attention systems to transformer models
- Connect neuroscience concepts to modern AI architectures

## Reflection

This lab gave me a much deeper understanding of how artificial intelligence systems process memory and attention compared to the human brain. Through multiple experiments, I was able to directly test how ChatGPT handled working memory, distraction, selective attention, and learning from examples. The lab connected neuroscience concepts from Modules 06 and 07 to the behavior of modern AI systems.

One of the most interesting findings was how the AI handled working memory tasks. Unlike humans, who can typically hold around four items in working memory, the AI was able to correctly recall information even when presented with very large lists. However, I learned that this does not represent true memory. Instead, the AI relies on its context window, meaning information is temporarily available during the conversation rather than permanently stored through consolidation like human memory.

The interference and forgetting experiment also demonstrated major differences between AI and biological memory systems. Humans rely on the hippocampus and consolidation processes to transfer memories into long-term storage. The AI, however, only retains information while it remains inside the active conversation context. Once the context is removed, the information is lost rather than biologically forgotten.

Another important concept explored in this lab was selective attention. The AI often focused on dominant topics while ignoring buried instructions or personally relevant information. This demonstrated similarities to bottom-up attention processes discussed in Module 07. However, the AI lacked the adaptive alerting and orienting systems found in humans, especially during the cocktail party effect experiment where it failed to prioritize personally relevant details.

The transformer attention visualization exercise was one of the strongest parts of the lab because it allowed me to directly observe how self-attention works inside a language model. Early layers focused on local word relationships while deeper layers captured larger contextual meaning and pronoun relationships. This closely paralleled the hierarchical attention systems in the brain, where lower sensory regions process simple features and higher cortical regions integrate more complex information.

Overall, this lab helped me better understand both the strengths and limitations of modern AI systems. While AI can demonstrate powerful attention and pattern-recognition abilities, it still lacks true consciousness, long-term biological memory, and adaptive personal relevance systems found in the human brain. The experiments also provided strong preparation for the midterm pilot audit project by showing how neuroscience concepts can be used to evaluate real-world AI behavior.
